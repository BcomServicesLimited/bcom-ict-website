Local landscape

What Burleigh Heads is actually like to work in

Burleigh has changed considerably in a decade and its commercial property has not entirely kept up. James Street is now one of the busiest dining and retail strips on the coast, and the premises along it were mostly built for something else — a shop, a house, a workshop — before being converted, sometimes more than once.

That produces a specific set of problems. Cabling is frequently improvised. Comms equipment ends up in a cupboard behind the coffee machine because there is nowhere else for it. Wireless has to cover through walls nobody planned around, including the original brick of a building that predates anyone thinking about signal.

The business mix is genuinely varied for a suburb this size. Hospitality along James Street and the highway, where a point of sale failure at service is the whole problem. Creative studios and agencies — design, media, marketing — typically small teams working across laptops and cloud tools. A growing layer of boutique professional practices: health and wellness, allied health, small legal and accounting firms attracted by the location. And retail through The Pines and the highway frontage.

Almost all of it is small — two to fifteen people, no internal IT, and often no provider at all until something breaks badly enough to force the issue.

What's technically different about Burleigh Heads

Converted premises are the defining constraint. Most commercial space here started as something else. There is rarely a proper comms room, cabling is often improvised or absent, and equipment ends up wherever there was a power point. We survey rather than assume, because what is actually in the walls determines what is possible and it varies enormously from one shopfront to the next.

Wireless has to work harder here. Original brick, added partitions, mezzanines and outdoor seating areas all fight coverage. A single consumer router covering a James Street venue including its terrace is not a realistic proposition, and no amount of turning it up changes that. It usually needs two properly placed access points and a cable run — which is a smaller job than people expect once someone has measured it.

Being small is not protective. A recurring conversation here. Almost everything that happens to small businesses is automated and indiscriminate — it finds whoever is reachable, and smaller operations are reachable precisely because the basics are missing. The invoice scam that redirects a payment does not care how many staff you have.

Remote support matters more at this distance. At twenty minutes out, the call-out is a meaningful proportion of a small job. We try remote first — no call-out on it — and book a visit only when the fault genuinely needs hands on hardware.

In practice

What IT support in Burleigh Heads actually involves

A representative engagement, drawn from real work with identifying detail removed — we don’t name clients without written permission.

Representative engagement

A Burleigh Heads shopfront with payments on the guest network

The situation

A business operating from a converted shopfront near James Street had card terminals dropping out during busy trade. It had been happening for months and was assumed to be the terminal provider’s problem.

What we found

Everything in the premises ran through one consumer router mounted behind the counter — the terminals, the customer WiFi, the music, and a tablet used for orders. Customer devices and payments were competing for the same capacity, and the busy period was exactly when both peaked. The building’s rendered walls also meant the single access point struggled to reach the rear of the tenancy.

What we did

Separated payments onto their own segment, put customer WiFi somewhere it cannot affect trading systems, and added coverage that reaches the back of the premises.

The outcome

Terminal dropouts stopped. Burleigh’s converted shopfronts and older buildings are frequently wired for a much smaller operation than the business now running in them.
