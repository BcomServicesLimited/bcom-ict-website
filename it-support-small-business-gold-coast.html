The awkward middle

You've outgrown the arrangement you've got

Almost every small business we take on arrives at the same point, from one of these directions.

The staff member who "knows computers"

Works fine until the business depends on it. Then someone whose actual job is something else is fixing the server instead, nothing is documented, and the business has a single point of failure who might resign.

A provider you can't get hold of

They were good when you were smaller. Now calls take days, nothing is proactive, and you have no idea whether your backups work — because nobody has ever tested one in front of you.

Nobody at all

Things get fixed when they break, by whoever is free. It works until a drive fails, an account gets compromised, or a client asks how you protect their information.

A scare

An email that nearly worked, a ransomware story from someone you know, or an insurer's renewal form that suddenly asks questions you can't answer.

What we'd look at first

  • Are your backups real? Not whether a backup exists — whether a restore has been tested, and whether ransomware could reach it from inside your network.
  • Is multi-factor authentication on everything? Usually it's on some accounts and not others, and the gap is where the problem comes from.
  • Is anything documented? Devices, licences, passwords, suppliers. Most businesses we take on have none of it written down, and it's the thing that hurts when someone leaves.
  • What's out of support? Unsupported operating systems and expired warranties on things you can't trade without.
  • What keeps breaking? Recurring faults usually mean an ageing fleet or an underlying cause nobody has chased.

Ad-hoc or managed

Both are legitimate and we will tell you honestly which suits you. Ad-hoc at $190 + GST per hour works when your setup is simple and an occasional problem is an annoyance rather than a crisis.

Managed IT makes sense once you have a server, staff who genuinely cannot work without their systems, or client data you would struggle to prove is protected. It is a flat monthly fee calculated from your requirements, month-to-month with no lock-in.

Plenty of our managed clients started as ad-hoc callers, and we have told plenty of businesses they are not ready yet. The free review is how we work out which you are.

Common problems

The problems we are actually called to in small businesses

Almost every small business we take on has at least four of these, and none of them are anybody’s fault.

“The person who set all this up has left”

Usually an arrangement that depended on one person — often a family member, a former staff member or a contractor — and no documentation of any kind.

What we do Recover and document what exists before touching it: accounts, licences, domain, hosting, network layout. Almost every small business we take on starts here, and it is a day’s work rather than a crisis.

“Nobody knows the passwords”

Usually credentials held in one person’s memory, browser or notebook. The business functions perfectly until the day it needs to change something.

What we do Establish proper ownership of the domain, the Microsoft tenancy and the accounting file, then hold credentials in a password manager the business controls. Ownership of the domain is the one worth checking first, because losing it is the hardest to undo.

“We all use the same login”

Usually convenience, usually dating from when the business had three people. It removes any ability to say who did what, and it means a departure requires changing a password everyone relies on.

What we do Give every person their own account with multi-factor authentication. This is the single highest-value hour of work in most small businesses and it costs nothing beyond the time.

“The computers are getting old but they still work”

Usually machines past the point where repair is economic. They usually fail one at a time, at random, and each failure costs a day nobody planned for.

What we do Plan replacement rather than reacting to it. Knowing that three machines are due next financial year turns an emergency into a line in a budget, and lets you buy well rather than urgently.

“We think the backup is working”

Usually a backup nobody has ever restored from. Backups fail silently, and the failure is discovered at the worst possible moment.

What we do Test a restore. Not a report saying the backup completed — an actual file, recovered. A backup is only a backup once somebody has got something back out of it.

“When something breaks we don’t know who to call”

Usually several suppliers with overlapping responsibilities and no agreement about who owns a problem. The internet provider, the software vendor and whoever built the website each point at the others.

What we do Have one number to call and let us deal with the rest. Most small business downtime is spent establishing whose problem it is rather than fixing it.

In practice

What this looks like in a small business

Representative engagements, drawn from real work with identifying detail removed — we don’t name clients without written permission.

Representative engagement

The domain nobody in the business owned

The situation

A small business of eleven people asked for help moving to Microsoft 365. Straightforward work, until the question of who controlled the domain name came up and nobody could answer it.

What we found

The domain had been registered fourteen years earlier by a web designer who had built the original site and had not been engaged since. It was registered in that designer’s own name, on their personal email address, with auto-renewal on a credit card that had long since expired. The business had no access to it whatsoever. Every email address the business used depended on a registration it did not own and could not renew.

What we did

Traced the registrar, established contact with the original designer, who transferred it willingly once asked, and moved the domain into an account owned by the business with the renewal on the business’s own card.

The outcome

The business now owns the thing its entire email identity rests on. Had that domain lapsed, the business would have lost every email address at once, and the recovery would have been a negotiation rather than an administrative task.

Representative engagement

Replacing computers on a plan instead of on a Tuesday

The situation

A business with fourteen staff had machines ranging from two to nine years old, bought as needed and never tracked. Failures were becoming frequent enough to be disruptive, and each one meant somebody idle for a day and a rushed purchase at retail prices.

What we found

Building an asset register found four machines past economic repair, three approaching it, and two already running an operating system no longer receiving security updates — which the business had not been told and had no way to know.

What we did

Set out a replacement schedule over eighteen months prioritising the unsupported machines, sourced at trade pricing with the configuration and data transfer handled before each machine was handed over, and recorded the fleet so the next cycle could be forecast rather than discovered.

The outcome

Replacement is now a budgeted line rather than a series of emergencies. The business also spends less per machine, because nothing is being bought on the afternoon it died.
