The sentence that costs businesses everything

“The NAS is our backup.”

It usually is not. A NAS mapped as a drive letter, on the same network, reachable with the same credentials, is a second copy of your data sitting in the blast radius of the first. Ransomware that reaches a workstation reaches it too. We have recovered a business whose backup unit was encrypted alongside the server it was protecting, because it was permanently mapped with the server’s own administrator credentials.

What turns a Synology into an actual backup is two settings and one habit: immutable snapshots so a version exists that encryption cannot overwrite, an offsite copy so fire and theft are covered, and a restore somebody has actually tested. None of them costs anything extra. Almost none of the units we inherit have all three.

Why Synology rather than a cheaper box

The hardware is unremarkable and that is fine — you are buying DSM. In particular you are buying Active Backup for Business, which backs up Windows machines, servers and your Microsoft 365 tenancy with no additional licence.

That last one matters more than most businesses realise. Microsoft replicates your data for their own availability; they do not keep a copy against you deleting something, a staff member leaving with a mailbox, or ransomware syncing through OneDrive. A Synology sitting in the cupboard covers that for the price of the box.

QNAP is a capable alternative and we support it. It frequently offers more hardware for the money. We deploy Synology more often because for a small business the software is the deciding factor, not the specification.

Common problems

What we find on an inherited NAS

“We back up to the NAS, so we are covered”

Usually a unit mapped as a drive letter on the same network with the same credentials. Ransomware that reaches a workstation reaches the backup too.

What we do Turn on immutable snapshots so a copy exists that encryption cannot overwrite, and add an offsite copy with Hyper Backup. A share you can open from a desk is storage, not backup.

“We have not checked it in years”

Usually a box in a cupboard doing its job silently, with a drive that failed eighteen months ago and an amber light nobody walks past.

What we do Set up notifications that actually reach someone, and check the drive health. A degraded array is fine until the second drive goes, and then it is not.

“It is full”

Usually capacity planned for the business three years ago. Design files, video and photo archives grow faster than anyone forecasts.

What we do Expand rather than replace where the unit allows it, and work out what should be on the NAS at all — a lot of what fills them is archive that belongs somewhere cheaper.

“Every share is open to every staff member”

Usually shares created quickly at setup with permissions nobody revisited, which is the same problem Microsoft 365 tenancies have.

What we do Set permissions by group against how the business actually works. It is an hour of work that answers a question a client or an insurer will eventually ask.

“We back up to it but not off it”

Usually a single copy in the same building as the originals. It survives a drive failure and not a fire, a theft or a flood.

What we do Add an offsite copy. Hyper Backup handles it, and the local copy stays for fast restores — see the comparison of NAS against cloud backup.

“It is reachable from the internet”

Usually QuickConnect or a port forward opened so someone could get files from home, still open and still indexed.

What we do Put it behind a VPN. Internet-exposed NAS units are actively scanned for and have been the entry point in real incidents.

A local copy is for speed and an offsite copy is for survival — they cover different failures and most businesses need both. See NAS vs cloud backup for how to decide, and backup and disaster recovery for the service.

In practice

What we find on a NAS nobody has opened

A representative engagement, drawn from real work with client and staff names removed.

Representative engagement

The drive that had been amber for eighteen months

The situation

A design business kept its entire project archive on a Synology in the office cupboard. It had been installed by a previous provider, it had never given any trouble, and nobody had opened the interface since the day it went in.

What we found

One drive in the array had failed roughly eighteen months earlier. The unit had done exactly what it was designed to do — carried on running in a degraded state on the remaining drives — and had been sending notifications to an email address belonging to the provider who installed it. A second drive failure at any point in those eighteen months would have taken the archive with it. Separately, nothing on the unit had ever left the building, and Microsoft 365 was not being backed up at all despite Active Backup for Business being available on the box and already paid for.

What we did

Replaced the failed drive and rebuilt the array, then set notifications to reach two people at the business rather than a departed supplier. Enabled immutable snapshots, configured an offsite copy with Hyper Backup, and turned on Active Backup for Business against the Microsoft 365 tenancy and the workstations.

The outcome

The archive is protected against a second drive failure, against fire and theft, and against ransomware overwriting the snapshots. Every one of those capabilities was already on the unit and already paid for. The only thing missing had been somebody opening it.

Common questions

Questions Gold Coast businesses ask us

Is a Synology NAS a backup?

Only if it is configured to be one. A NAS mapped as a drive on the same network with the same credentials is storage — ransomware that reaches a workstation reaches it too, and we have recovered from an incident where the backup unit was encrypted alongside the server it was protecting. Immutable snapshots and an offsite copy are what turn a NAS into a backup.

Which Synology model suits a small business?

For most Gold Coast small businesses the desktop plus range — DS224+ through DS923+ — covers it, and the deciding factors are how much data you hold, how fast it is growing, and whether you want to run Active Backup for Business against servers and workstations. Rack units make sense once there is a comms cabinet and the data has outgrown a desktop box.

Synology or QNAP?

Both are capable and the hardware is comparable. Synology's advantage is DSM and Active Backup for Business, which backs up PCs, servers and Microsoft 365 without an additional licence — for most small businesses that is the deciding factor. QNAP often gives more hardware for the money and suits a business that wants raw capability or specific virtualisation features. We support both; we deploy Synology more often for the software.

Can a Synology back up Microsoft 365?

Yes, through Active Backup for Business, and it is one of the strongest reasons to have one. Microsoft replicates your data for their availability but does not keep a copy against deletion, a departing staff member, or ransomware syncing through OneDrive. Almost no business has this until someone explains it.

Should our NAS be accessible from the internet?

Not directly. Put it behind a VPN rather than exposing it through a port forward or QuickConnect. Internet-facing NAS units are actively scanned for, and they have been the entry point in real ransomware incidents in Australia.

Can you take over a NAS someone else set up?

Yes. We recover administrative access, review the share permissions and the backup jobs against what the business actually needs, check drive health, and set up notifications that reach a person. Inherited units are where we most often find a failed drive nobody knew about.
